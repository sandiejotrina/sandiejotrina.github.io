Sample 1 · Prescribe

RAPID: what to automate

Score a candidate process from 1 to 4 on each of five questions. Total out of 20.

Open the interactive RAPID scorer

R · Repetitive
Does it happen often, the same way each time?
A · Accuracy-critical
Do errors carry real cost, risk, or rework?
P · Predictable
Are the inputs and decision rules stable and clear?
I · Integration-heavy
Does someone move data between systems by hand?
D · Documented
Is the process written down well enough to build from?
  • 15 to 20

    Automate

    Strong candidate. Move to tool selection.

  • 10 to 14

    Not ready

    Document and standardize the process first, then score again.

  • Under 10

    Redesign or leave to people

    Automation would add cost without adding value.

The guardrail: a high score tells you a process can be automated. It does not tell you it should be. The highest-scoring process is sometimes a broken one you would only be paving over. Diagnose first.

Sample 4 · Treat

The AI review gate

A pattern for using generative AI where accuracy and accountability matter: training content, client communication, regulated correspondence.

  1. Approved sources only. The AI drafts from a defined knowledge base, not the open internet.
  2. De-identified test data. Prove it works before real data touches it.
  3. Documented prompts. A prompt library with versions, so results can be repeated and audited.
  4. Human review before release. A named person approves every output. Nothing goes out on the AI's authority.
  5. Measure the work, not the tool. Turnaround time, error rate, and adoption, compared with the old process.

See it in practice.

Sample 5 · Govern

My AI governance practice, mapped to NIST AI RMF

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework organizes AI risk work into four functions. This is how the controls I already run in a regulated Medical Affairs function line up with them. It is a practitioner's mapping, not a certification claim.

Govern
Who is accountable, and what rules apply
A named owner for every AI workflow. Business, IT, Legal, Quality, and clinical stakeholders sign off before anything reaches production. Every build is documented well enough for someone else to run and audit it.
Map
Understand the use and its risk
Every request goes through intake. The workflow is diagnosed before any tool is chosen, RAPID scores whether it should be automated, and the data involved is classified so sensitive information never reaches a test environment.
Measure
Test and track performance
Proof of concept on de-identified data first. AI output is checked against a known answer, such as procedure outcome data, before go-live. Turnaround, accuracy, and adoption are tracked against the old process.
Manage
Control and respond in production
A human review gate before any output leaves the team. Prompts are versioned in a library so changes are deliberate and traceable. The audit trail meets FDA 21 CFR Part 11 expectations for regulated records.

The point: in regulated work, governance is not paperwork added at the end. It is designed into the workflow from intake, which is why these systems got approved and used.

Sample 6 · Follow Up

A certification path that proves readiness

One structure for any field or client-facing role. Certified means able to do the work, not finished with the modules.

  1. Foundation. The problem the product solves and who it serves. Knowledge check.
  2. Product. How it works and how it is delivered. Knowledge check.
  3. Risk. What can go wrong and how to respond. Scenario assessment.
  4. Compliance. The rules of the role, documented. Attestation on file.
  5. Readiness. Observed role play and a supervised live interaction. Signed off by a manager.
  6. Stay current. Recertify when the product, data, or rules change, not just on a calendar date.

The metric that matters: time to readiness and pass rates by role, not course completions.

Sample 7 · Follow Up

The AI value scorecard

Most AI programs report licenses and logins. Leaders need to know whether the work changed. Six measures, set before launch and tracked after it.

1 · Baseline
Measure the current process before building anything: volume, cycle time, hours, errors. No baseline, no credible result.
2 · Adoption
Share of the eligible work that now runs through the new workflow. Usage, not access.
3 · Capacity returned
Hours of manual work removed each year, and what the team now does with that time.
4 · Speed
Cycle time and turnaround, from request to resolution.
5 · Quality and risk
Error and rework rates, audit findings, and whether records are complete when they are created.
6 · Cost
Three-year total cost of ownership against the value above, compared with the alternatives that were not chosen.

Applied to a real program

A grants and investigator-initiated studies portfolio modernization, scored on the measures that were tracked:

2,300+manual hours returned each year (capacity)
~1/3lower three-year cost than the custom build (cost)
4 vs 13months to implement, bought versus built (speed)
Audit readycompliant records replaced manual, non-compliant work (quality and risk)

The rule: agree on the scorecard before launch, with the people who will judge the result. Value you define afterward is just a story.
